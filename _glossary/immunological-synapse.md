The immunological synapse is the organized interface — comprising a central supramolecular activation cluster (cSMAC) and a peripheral SMAC (pSMAC) — formed between an immune cell and its target or antigen-presenting cell during receptor engagement.

It is the site where trogocytosis occurs: ligand-receptor engagement at the synapse triggers signaling (Lck/Syk → PI3K → actin remodeling in T cells; Src-kinase/Ca2+/PKC in NK cells) that drives membrane transfer between the two cells.

It is also where cytotoxic lymphocytes deliver their killing machinery, secreting granzyme proteases and the pore-forming protein perforin into the interface to elicit programmed cell death in the target. The synapse responds not only to the biochemical composition of the target cell but also to its mechanical properties, because several of the activating immunoreceptors that build it — the T cell antigen receptor, certain activating NK receptors, and integrins such as LFA-1 — bind ligand and signal optimally only when placed under tension.
